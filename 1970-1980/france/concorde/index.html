Concorde

Manufacturer: Aérospatiale (France) and British Aircraft Corporation (United Kingdom)
Country / decade: France, 1970–1980
Category: Civil
Role: Supersonic airliner
First flight: 2 March 1969
Entered service: 21 January 1976, with Air France and British Airways

Design and development

Concorde's delta wing and drooping nose were both direct engineering responses to the demands of sustained supersonic flight: the delta shape provided stability across the wide speed range from takeoff to Mach 2, while the hinged nose let it tilt downward for visibility during takeoff and landing, since the aircraft's high-speed profile otherwise blocked the pilots' forward view at low speed. Development costs are estimated to have run to roughly £1.3 billion, split between the British and French governments — a sum so large, relative to any realistic return from ticket sales, that both governments effectively treated the programme as a national prestige investment rather than a conventional commercial one.

Where it was used, and by whom

Air France flew Concorde from Paris–Charles de Gaulle, principally on transatlantic routes, while British Airways flew it from London Heathrow; both airlines added service to Washington Dulles in 1976 and New York JFK in 1977. Only twenty aircraft were built in total, of which fourteen entered commercial airline service.

Notes

Concorde was the product of a 1962 Anglo-French government treaty and remains the clearest symbol of European aerospace collaboration in this period, cruising at more than twice the speed of sound and cutting transatlantic flight time by roughly half.
