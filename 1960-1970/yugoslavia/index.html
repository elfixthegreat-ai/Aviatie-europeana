Yugoslavia · 1960–1970

Yugoslavia occupied a unique position in Cold War Europe: after Marshal Tito's 1948 break with Stalin, the country pursued a policy of non-alignment, refusing to depend militarily on either NATO or the Warsaw Pact and becoming a founding member of the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961. That political stance shaped its aircraft industry directly — rather than buy or license trainers from either bloc, Yugoslavia built its own.

SOKO, established in 1950 in Mostar from the relocated aircraft division of the Ikarus company, produced the G-2 Galeb, Yugoslavia's first jet trainer, which first flew in 1961. A single-seat attack derivative, the J-21 Jastreb, followed in 1965, giving Yugoslavia both a trainer and a light attack type of entirely domestic design.

Yugoslavia's non-aligned position occasionally put it in an unusual bridging role between the two blocs: Yugoslav-built jet engines were supplied to Warsaw Pact member Poland for its TS-11 Iskra prototype in 1960, at a time when NATO supplier Britain would not sell the preferred engine directly to a communist state — a small but telling example of how Yugoslavia's independence let it trade across the Cold War divide in ways neither bloc's own members could.
