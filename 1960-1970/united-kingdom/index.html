United Kingdom · 1960–1970

The fragmentation of the 1950s gave way to consolidation. In 1960, English Electric, Vickers-Armstrongs, Bristol Aircraft, and Hunting Aircraft merged into the British Aircraft Corporation (BAC), while the Hawker Siddeley Group brought together Hawker, Armstrong Whitworth, Avro, Gloster, and de Havilland under one roof. Government pressure to stop duplicating development costs across half a dozen small firms was a major driver.

Militarily, the decade belonged to the English Electric Lightning, the only all-British fighter ever to reach Mach 2 in service, and to Britain's continuing role at the front line of NATO air defence. On the civil side, BAC's One-Eleven became the second short-haul jet airliner in the world after France's Caravelle, and found real success in the American market — a rare case of a British airliner selling well in the United States.

In 1962, Britain and France signed the treaty that would launch Concorde, committing both governments to a supersonic airliner programme neither could easily have justified alone. It would take most of the next decade to reach the runway.
