West Germany · 1960–1970

Rearmament proceeded at real scale in this decade, and nowhere was that clearer than in the Luftwaffe's adoption of the American-designed Lockheed F-104 Starfighter, built under license by a consortium of European manufacturers led in Germany by Messerschmitt and Dornier. West Germany became the largest single operator of the type anywhere in the world, acquiring roughly a third of total worldwide production — a scale of investment that reflected West Germany's position as NATO's likely front line in any conflict with the Warsaw Pact.

German industry itself consolidated to support this expansion: in 1968–69, Messerschmitt merged with Bölkow and the helicopter and space divisions of Hamburger Flugzeugbau to form Messerschmitt-Bölkow-Blohm (MBB), a single company built with scale — and NATO-wide programmes — in mind. That same consolidation logic extended across borders: the Transall C-160 transport, launched jointly with France in 1959 and in service from 1967, was one of the first tangible results of West German industry working as an equal partner with a wartime adversary rather than as a supplier or customer.

By the end of the decade, West Germany had gone from prohibition to becoming one of NATO's largest aircraft operators in barely fifteen years.
