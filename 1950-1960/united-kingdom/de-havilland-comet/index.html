de Havilland Comet

Manufacturer: de Havilland Aircraft Company
Country / decade: United Kingdom, 1950–1960
Category: Civil
Role: Jet airliner — the world's first to enter commercial service
First flight: 27 July 1949 (prototype)
Entered service: 2 May 1952, with BOAC

Design and development

The Comet's design broke genuinely new ground: no manufacturer had previously built a pressurised cabin for sustained high-altitude jet travel, and de Havilland's engineers had no real precedent to draw on for how metal fatigue would behave under the repeated pressurisation cycles of daily airline service. The 1954 accidents, later traced to fatigue cracks starting at the corners of the aircraft's rectangular windows, prompted the industry-wide shift toward the rounded window and door openings still standard on every jet airliner today.

Where it was used, and by whom

British Overseas Airways Corporation (BOAC) was the launch and principal operator, flying the Comet from London to Johannesburg, Singapore, and Tokyo. After a redesign, the Comet 4 made the first scheduled transatlantic jet crossing in October 1958. Later operators included British European Airways, Olympic Airways, Aerolíneas Argentinas, East African Airways, Middle East Airlines, Mexicana, and the charter airline Dan-Air, which flew Comets into the 1970s. A military derivative became the Hawker Siddeley Nimrod maritime patrol aircraft, which served the RAF until 2011.

Notes

The Comet pioneered the pressurised jet cabin for airline passengers but suffered a series of catastrophic in-flight structural failures in 1953-54, traced to metal fatigue around its window frames. The entire fleet was grounded and the aircraft substantially redesigned before the improved Comet 4 restored Britain's early jet-age lead — briefly, before the larger American Boeing 707 and Douglas DC-8 overtook it commercially.
