United Kingdom · 1950–1960

Britain entered the 1950s with an unusually fragmented industry by later standards: Hawker, de Havilland, Vickers, English Electric, Avro, Gloster, and Bristol all designed and built aircraft independently, each competing for the same government contracts. That fragmentation did not stop Britain becoming, for a few years, the world's most advanced civil jet manufacturer, when the de Havilland Comet entered service in 1952 as the first jet airliner anywhere to carry fare-paying passengers.

The Comet's early lead was cut short by a series of fatal structural failures in 1954, which grounded the type and forced a substantial redesign — a sobering lesson in the unknowns of pressurised jet flight that the whole industry, not just Britain, had to absorb. On the military side, Cold War tension and NATO commitments kept British factories busy supplying first-generation jet fighters such as the Hawker Hunter to the RAF and to allied air forces across Europe and the Commonwealth.

By the end of the decade Britain still led Western Europe in jet expertise, but the commercial ground lost during the Comet's grounding would not be regained: American manufacturers, less encumbered by the Comet's early mistakes, were about to overtake Britain's civil lead with larger, longer-ranged jets of their own.

Aircraft types covered
